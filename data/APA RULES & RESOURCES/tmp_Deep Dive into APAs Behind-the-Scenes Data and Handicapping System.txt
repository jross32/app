Hidden Stats and “Backstage” Data in the APA API
The APA League app’s API  can expose certain player statistics and details that are not directly visible on the
public member site. In your api_dump_v2.json  (and similar data files), there are fields that amount to
“backstage” data  – extra stats and info the app fetches behind the scenes. For example, the API returns
Lifetime Stats  for players with fields like a player’s matches played , matches won , “CLA” , defensive shot
average , etc. . The CLA field (Current Lifetime Average) is an internal skill metric – essentially a numeric
value corresponding to the player’s true average skill. In one example from the dump, a player had a CLA of
7  in  both  8-ball  and  9-ball,  indicating  a  high-skilled  player .  Likewise,  the  API  provides  a
defensiveShotAvg  – the average number of defensive shots the player makes per match (e.g. 1.86 in 8-
ball, 2.29 in 9-ball for that player) . These metrics are  not shown on the public APA site or standings
pages, but they are part of the data the app uses internally (likely visible to you only because you captured
the raw JSON).
In addition to lifetime summary stats, the API data can include  special achievement counts  and other
“hidden” stats. For instance, the JSON structure tracks accomplishments like  8-on-the-Break, Break-and-
Run, Rackless nights, 9-on-the-Snap, etc.  (often used for patches and awards) even if the public site
doesn’t list them prominently . These indicate how many times a player achieved certain feats, which is
typically  back-end data  used for patch awards. There are also fields like  matchCountForLastTwoYrs
(matches played in the last 2 years) and lastPlayed  date for each player’s lifetime record  – again,
info mainly for internal/reference use. In short, the JSON dump is indeed a more comprehensive dataset
than what a regular user sees on APA’s website. It’s pulling together all the league structure (divisions,
teams,  rosters)  plus  detailed  stats  and  history  for  each  player .  This  “behind-the-scenes”  data  isn’t
classified or secret per se , but it’s not displayed in the normal member UI. It’s accessible through the API
calls – essentially backstage pass  information that the official app uses to populate certain screens (like your
personal stats page) but might not be readily visible or user-facing without digging.
However , it’s important to note that while these extra stats (CLA, defensive averages, lifetime totals, etc.) are
internally available , they don’t reveal the APA’s proprietary handicap formula itself . They are more like
supporting data. For example, CLA is likely an internal rating or a reflection of your skill level calculation,
and APA even references it in their manual: “No player may play below their CLA (Current Lifetime Average)” ,
meaning a player’s handicap won’t be allowed to stay below what their cumulative performance suggests
. So the API gave you some insight into each player’s underlying metrics (probably what APA’s system
uses to evaluate your skill), but it doesn’t directly hand out the formula or any truly “top-secret” numbers
beyond those stats. Think of it this way: your data dump is like reading the  box scores  and averages,
whereas the “secret sauce” is how those numbers decide a skill level – and that algorithm isn’t explicitly in
the JSON.1

Accessing “Top-Secret” APA Data (Skill Level Calculation)
It sounds like you’re looking for the actual handicap calculation formula or administrative data  that APA
keeps under wraps – essentially the  “top secret APA data”  on how skill levels are determined. The APA’s
handicap  system  (called  The  Equalizer® )  is  indeed  formula-driven ,  not  an  arbitrary  decision  by  one
person. But APA intentionally keeps the exact formula confidential , to prevent players from gaming the
system. Even league operators get only a general understanding. The  good news  is that over the years,
league members and some insiders have reverse-engineered or described the system’s outlines. The skill
level (SL) formula for APA 8-ball  can be summarized (in simplified terms) as being based on your average
innings per game , with adjustments for defensive shots and other factors . In other words, the
system looks at how many turns (innings) it takes for you to win games (or how you perform in losses), and
the fewer innings you need, the higher your skill level. Defensive shots  come into play by subtracting  those
innings from the total – since a marked defensive shot indicates a deliberate safety play, it shouldn’t count
as a “turn” that makes you look worse. As one player explains,  “Innings minus defensive shots”  is the key
metric; if scorekeepers don’t mark safeties, it will appear you took more innings and thus could keep your
skill lower . This is why APA harps on properly marking defensive shots – it directly impacts handicaps.
APA’s  official  literature  confirms  that  wins  and  losses  alone  are  not  what  determine  skill  –  it’s  the
performance data on the scoresheet  that matters . In fact, you can lose a match but still go up  in
skill level if you played extremely well (e.g. a close match with very few innings) – conversely, you might win
but not go up if it took many innings or the opponent was much lower skill. The  APA Team Manual
mentions that the League Operator regularly reviews and updates skill levels using  “previous information
recorded on your weekly scoresheet” , and it notes that several good scores (best performances) are weighted
more to lend stability . Typically, APA uses a player’s  best 10 of their last 20 match scores  to
calculate the current SL  – this prevents one bad night from dragging you down or one great night from
immediately bumping you up too far . It’s a rolling evaluation of consistency.
So what exactly is the  “top secret” formula?  While APA won’t publish it, the community consensus (and
leaked info) indicates it’s fairly complex but systematic . For 8-ball, a commonly cited model is: calculate the
average innings per winning game (with safeties removed), then map that to a skill level range . For example,
Skill Level 7  corresponds to an average of about 2 innings per game or less , SL6 might be ~2–3 innings,
SL5 around 3–4 innings, and so on, down to SL2 which can be 7+ innings . These ranges have been
documented by players and even appear in Dr . Dave’s analysis of the APA Equalizer system. To prevent
sandbagging  (intentionally  inflating  your  innings  to  stay  low),  APA  also  employs  an  “applied  score”
mechanism. If a player with a high win percentage tries to pad their innings, the system will override that
raw score with an applied  innings value based on their win % . In simpler terms, if you keep winning
a lot, the algorithm assumes you should be playing at a higher skill and won’t let a few drawn-out games
fool it. An insider explanation notes:  “The APA system will give you an ‘applied score’ based on your winning
percentage… it’s next to impossible to drop a skill level while maintaining above a 50% win rate” . For
instance, a player winning 90% of their matches will have an automatic performance floor (the system
might treat their match as if it were done in a very low number of innings, no matter how many innings they
actually took) . This ensures frequent winners don’t stay at a lower SL just by dragging games out.
Conversely, if you lose often, the system uses your actual performance (no “help” from applied scores), so
you can drop if your game warrants it. The end result  is a handicap that reflects your true ability as closely
as possible, using a combination of mathematical averaging and business rules  (like the applied score
and “best 10 of 20” rule). It’s definitely not a manual or whimsical process – APA’s computer software does
the heavy lifting based on the data input.78

From  an  administrative  data  standpoint,  APA  does  have  layers  of  review  on  handicaps.  The  League
Operator  (LO)  in  each  area  monitors  for  accuracy  and  fairness.  The  manual  even  suggests  having  a
Handicap Advisory Committee (HAC)  to watch for sandbagging or abnormal changes . And if a player’s
calculated skill (their CLA) seems too high or low due to exceptional circumstances, the LO can appeal to
the APA Handicap Review Committee (HRC)  for an adjustment . These are internal governance steps –
again, not publicly visible – to maintain the integrity of the system. None of this info is exposed via the
member API or normal site, but it’s how APA “decisions” on handicaps are made. So, in summary: yes, there
is a formula (with multiple factors like innings, defensive shots, win %, recent trends)  for how players
level  up  or  down.  It’s  not  a  simple  one-line  formula  you  can  query  from  the  API,  and  APA  keeps  it
proprietary. What you can see (and you have, through your data dump) are the inputs and outputs of that
formula – the number of innings, defensive shot counts, win/loss records, and the resulting skill levels and
CLAs. The actual computational logic stays on APA’s side of the wall (their servers/software). There isn’t a
magic “conspiracy data” file with the formula – it’s baked into their code.
League Operator Accounts and Different Access Levels
You also asked whether League Operator or Franchisee accounts see a different APA structure , and if
so, how you could view that. The answer is yes – League Operators have an administrative interface and
data access  that regular members do not. In the current APA system, a League Operator can manage
divisions, input scores, and adjust player info using either a special online portal or the admin mode of the
software. For example, when weekly play scoresheets are turned in (or submitted via the app), the LO uses a
tool (the old system was a program called ARES/APA Scorekeeper , and now possibly an admin panel in the
new app) to enter the scores – including innings and safeties – for each match. The LO’s interface  will show
all the granular data (every player’s scoresheet details, points, innings, etc.) and then compute the updates.
It’s essentially the back-end dashboard  for league management. As a player , you cannot normally see the
full scoresheet or those inning details after submission  (the app only shows you a summary) .
This is by design – APA sends the detailed data to their national system, calculates ratings, and only returns
the outcomes (updated handicaps, points, etc.) for members to see. An LO, however ,  can pull up past
scoresheets, match details, and even reports of players’ performance metrics that players don’t have direct
access to.
For instance, some LO-level reports might show a player’s  average innings per match, their defensive
shot count, or even a list of their best scores . LOs also see indicators if a player is “on the bubble”  of
moving up or has a red flag for sandbagging. The data you obtained via the API is likely the most you can
get  as  a  standard  member  login.  To  get  the  true  “admin  view,”  you  would  need  credentials  with  LO
privileges. It’s not just a different JSON file or a hidden URL – it’s a permission level on APA’s systems. The
structure of the data might be the same  (since the app is pulling from the same database), but certain
queries  or  endpoints  will  simply  refuse  data  if  you’re  not  authorized.  For  example,  there  may  be
administrative endpoints for roster moves, manual skill adjustments, or finance and membership info that
are completely inaccessible without an LO login. Scraping with a different member’s account won’t help
unless that member  is an LO or has higher permissions. Even a team captain or division rep login is
essentially the same as a normal member in terms of data access (the APA app doesn’t distinguish those
roles for data visibility, aside from maybe allowing a captain to score matches).
In practice, the only way you’d “see” the LO view is if you became a League Operator (or convinced one to let
you peek). APA is a franchised business, and LOs use either a dedicated League Management website  or
the APA League Operator application  to run things. For example, APA’s manual notes that “Local League19
